Your task: take a clear position on a future-of-work issue and persuade your audience to act. Explain what should change, who should make the change, and why your evidence supports it.

## 1. What you need to do

- Make one specific, arguable claim about the future of work. A reasonable person should be able to disagree with it.
- Support your claim with **3 researched, credible sources in total**. Include both primary and secondary sources: at least one of each. Cite all three out loud.
- Use **ethos, pathos, or logos** effectively. You may use more than one appeal.
- Explain and answer at least one strong counterargument.
- Give clear, realistic recommendations for a named audience.
- Use **3 to 4 content slides**. Add a separate APA references slide and, if you used AI, an AI Use Statement slide. These extra slides do not count toward the 3 to 4 content slides.
- Speak for **4 to 5 minutes**. Q&A is additional time. Answer at least one audience question.

You do not need to cover a whole issue. A focused, well-supported argument is stronger than a rushed list of ideas.

## 3. Choose a topic and build your claim

Your presentation answers the course question: **What will work look like in the future, and how can I prepare for it?** Choose one of the topics below or propose your own course-related topic.

Claim formula: **[Who] should [do what] because [main reason].**

| Too broad or descriptive | Specific and arguable |
| --- | --- |
| AI is changing work. | NYU Shanghai should require a first-year AI literacy module because students need to evaluate AI output in every field. |
| Workers need new skills. | Employers should fund reskilling when automation changes employees' jobs because workers should not bear the full cost alone. |

- **AI and jobs:** entry-level positions; changing tasks rather than disappearing jobs; AI and care work; human review in AI hiring; who should pay for reskilling.
- **Learning and careers:** required AI literacy; skills and portfolios versus degree names; student-led inquiry; transferable skills; academic recognition for CCD career-development activities.
- **What counts as work:** unpaid care; a four-day workweek; protections for gig workers; meaningful work versus salary.
- **Ethics and technology:** workplace surveillance; deepfake verification; the right to disconnect; the value of human judgment.
- **Sustainability:** green skills for all majors; AI energy and water use; remote or hybrid work.
- **Employability:** AI-assisted applications; professional online presence; human interviews versus AI video interviews.

Course starting points include the readings, Videos 1 to 7, optional ethics and technology listening, and your CCD learning. A topic is not a claim: narrow it to one action for one audience. CCD visits are career-development experiences; this course has no internship requirement.

## 4. Research: three sources, both types

Use **3 sources in total**, not three primary sources plus three secondary sources. Include at least one primary source and at least one secondary source; the third can be either. At least one source should come from EAP 100 course materials.

| Source type | What it means | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Original evidence you can examine directly. | A research article reporting its own study; official statistics; a policy you analyze; an interview or survey you conduct with consent. |
| Secondary | A source that explains, interprets, or evaluates original evidence. | A review article; an analytical news report; an expert explanation that discusses other people's research. |

Classify a source by what it does. A TED talk or organizational report is not automatically primary or secondary. A report presenting its own data can be primary; a report summarizing other studies can be secondary. If you are unsure, ask before relying on it.

You do not have to conduct your own survey or interview. Published original evidence can meet the primary-source requirement. If you collect evidence yourself, get consent, protect privacy, and avoid sensitive personal information.

- Check who produced each source, when it was published, what evidence it uses, and how directly it supports your claim.
- Cite every source out loud when you use it. Example: "According to the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025..."
- Put a short citation beside borrowed data, images, or ideas on slides. Include full APA references on the references slide and in your submitted list.
- AI chatbots are not research sources. Find and verify the original source for every statistic or quotation. MyBib can help with APA formatting, but check its output.

## 5. Persuade ethically: ethos, pathos, or logos

| Appeal | What it looks like | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethos: credibility | Use trustworthy evidence, explain your relevant knowledge, and represent opposing views fairly. | Claiming expertise you do not have or treating a famous person's opinion as proof. |
| Pathos: values and emotions | Use a relevant human example to show why the issue matters to your audience. | Invented stories presented as facts, exaggeration, or manipulating emotions. |
| Logos: reasoning and evidence | Connect a reason to credible evidence, then explain why that evidence supports your claim. | Dropping in a statistic without explanation or claiming that correlation proves causation. |

Use at least one of these appeals effectively. You do not have to announce "this is ethos." Your audience should experience the appeal through your choices of evidence, examples, and reasoning.

**Counterargument:** explain the strongest reasonable objection, then respond with evidence or reasoning. You can acknowledge a real limitation without giving up your main argument.

## 6. Organize and time your speech

Use two main reasons to keep the argument focused. This example totals **4 minutes 30 seconds**, excluding Q&A; you may adjust it as long as your speech stays within 4 to 5 minutes.

| Part | What to do | Example time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | Hook the audience, establish relevance and credibility, state your claim, and preview your reasons. | 30 seconds |
| Reason 1 | Give a reason, cite evidence, and explain how it supports the claim. | 60 seconds |
| Reason 2 | Give a different reason, cite evidence, and explain the connection. | 60 seconds |
| Counterargument and response | State the strongest objection fairly and answer it. | 45 seconds |
| Recommendations | Give 2 to 3 specific actions and name who should take them. | 45 seconds |
| Conclusion | Restate your claim and finish with a clear call to action. | 30 seconds |
| Q&A | Answer at least one audience question. | After the speech |

Useful signposts: "Today I'm arguing that..." / "My first reason is..." / "This evidence matters because..." / "A reasonable objection is..." / "However..." / "I recommend..."

Before presentation day, prepare three likely audience questions, including one that challenges your claim. Practice answers of about 30 to 45 seconds. It is fine to say you do not know; do not invent an answer. Clarify or qualify your claim when the evidence warrants it.

## 7. Make slides that help, not distract

Prepare **3 to 4 content slides**, then a separate APA references slide. If applicable, put your AI Use Statement immediately after the references.

| Content slide | Suggested job |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Show the hook, issue, and claim. |
| 2 | Show key evidence for your reasons, with short citations. |
| 3 | Show the counterargument, your response, and your recommendations. |
| Optional 4 | Separate the counterargument from the recommendations or give complex evidence its own space. |

This is a suggested layout, not a required template. Use one clear idea per slide, readable fonts, and minimal text. Do not paste your script onto the slides. Check that charts, labels, and citations can be read from the back of the room.
