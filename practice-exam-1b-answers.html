Section B · 12 marks

B1 [4] The real worry is the speed and acceleration of change (1). His history tour shows that job loss has happened in every era, from hunters to farmers to blacksmiths (1), and that old jobs were replaced by more new, specialized ones (1). So he reframes disruption as a normal part of human progress, even if fast change feels uncomfortable (1).
B2 [4] Claim: work in the future will look like leisure to us, just as our jobs would look like leisure to people from the past (1). Example (1): any of: being paid to play video games, eat at restaurants, travel, or talk to friends on video (podcasting). Humor (2): the “podcast bros” joke makes the point memorable and relatable, lowers the audience’s anxiety, and supports his optimistic, non-fearful tone.
B3 [4] Shared point (2): confident predictions about which jobs will disappear are often wrong (programming did not vanish; chess grew after AI won). Advice (2): do not let fear of job-loss predictions stop you from pursuing what you are passionate about; humans are good at creating meaning and purpose.

Exemplar (Section C)

In his 2025 TED talk, Vlad Tenev argues that although AI may replace most of today’s jobs, history shows that job disruption has always been followed by an even faster creation of new work, which he calls a “job singularity.” For this reason, he believes young people should not let fear of automation shape their career choices. This connects closely to our Week 1 question, “What counts as work?”

Both Tenev and the Week 1 readings agree that the definition of work is not fixed. Tenev notes that many of our jobs, such as playing video games professionally, would not have looked like real work to earlier generations. Similarly, the Week 1 readings show that care work, civic work, and emotional labor are real work even when they are not recognized or paid.

However, there is a genuine tension. Tenev’s argument focuses on new paid jobs and entrepreneurship, such as one-person companies. The Week 1 readings, in contrast, highlight invisible and unpaid labor that markets often ignore. If AI creates many new paid roles, it is not clear that care and civic work will gain more value or recognition. Tenev’s optimism may therefore apply mainly to people who can access technology and markets.

Overall, Tenev offers a hopeful view of change, but the Week 1 readings remind us to ask who benefits and which kinds of work still go unseen.
