1. A familiar face

Ads: Oprah Winfrey’s “O, That’s Good!” food line · a champion athlete’s sportswear ad · Martin Scorsese’s MasterClass

PREDICTHow might a famous person promoting food influence an audience?

Strong answers: we trust people we know, so we trust what they recommend. Fans may want to copy the celebrity’s lifestyle. A famous face also gets attention and makes the product seem popular or high quality.

WATCHMain appeal + a detail that proves it

  • Food ad: Ethos Oprah presents the food as her personal recommendation: When you find something great, I always want to share it. We trust it because we trust her. (The nutritious twist line adds a little logos.)
  • Athlete ad: Ethos Pathos His record is his credibility: It doesn’t matter how many records I break. Dramatic music and the best version of myself make you feel driven, too. Either appeal is correct with evidence.
  • Filmmaking class: Ethos I’m Martin Scorsese and this is my MasterClass. He is one of the most respected film directors alive, so a class taught by him feels worth paying for. There is pathos, too: if you feel like you can’t rest until you’ve told this story… speaks to your passion.

CHALLENGEIf you didn’t recognize the person, would it still persuade you?

Mostly no. Ethos only works when the audience knows the person, and the food ad gives few other reasons to buy. The athlete and Scorsese ads still work a little, because their words and music create emotion.

For your speech: don’t assume your audience knows your source. Say who they are and why they’re credible: “According to a 2025 report by the World Economic Forum…”

2. Make the audience care

Ad: a car-safety commercial (Volvo). A mother walks her daughter to her first day of school.

PREDICTWhat emotion might an advertiser connect with the first day of school?

Strong answers: nervousness, hope, pride, nostalgia, or a parent’s love and worry.

WATCHThe feeling, and the detail that creates it

Pathos Feeling: tenderness and hope, with a little worry.

  • The mother comforts her: Everyone’s a little bit nervous on the first day of school.
  • The girl shares her life plan, which jumps ahead to her future: make some new friends… study hard… my own family. And my daughter.
  • Soft music and close-ups of the two faces.

LOOK CLOSERWhat does the ad want you to believe or do? What’s missing?

In this clip, you can’t tell. There’s no product, no brand, and no claim, only a feeling. That’s the lesson: emotion gets attention, but on its own it doesn’t tell the audience what to think or do.

In the full ad, the girl steps in front of a car and its automatic braking stops it in time. The message: Volvo’s safety technology protects the people you love, so buy a Volvo. The logos (the safety feature) is what connects the feeling to the product.

For your speech: if you use a story to make people care, follow it right away with your claim and your evidence. A feeling without a point is just a nice moment.

3. Numbers sound convincing

Ad: Crest Glamorous White Whitestrips

PREDICTWhat evidence would convince you that one whitening product works better?

Strong answers: clinical test results, before-and-after photos, approval from dentists or a dental association, a percentage or comparison, or an independent study (not one paid for by the company).

WATCHOne claim or comparison + main appeal

  • Whiten 25 times better than a leading whitening toothpaste.
  • The only ADA-accepted whitening strips proven to be safe and effective.

Logos A number and a comparison sound like scientific proof. The ADA (American Dental Association) mention adds ethos. The opening joke, Clearly, my whitening toothpaste is not cutting it, uses a little pathos (embarrassment about your smile).

QUESTION THE CLAIMWhat would you check before accepting it?

  • “25 times better” at what? How was whiteness measured, and after how long?
  • Which toothpaste? “A leading” one is never named, so you can’t compare.
  • Who ran the test? Was it Crest’s own research, or independent? How many people?
  • Is the ADA acceptance current, and what exactly does it cover?
For your speech: a number only persuades a careful audience if you say where it comes from and what it measures. Name the source, the year, and the comparison.

4. Your turn: analyze without help

Ads: TrueCar (car shopping) · POM Wonderful (pomegranate juice)

PREDICTExpert, explanation, or feeling: which persuades you more?

Any choice is fine if your why is specific. For example: “A clear explanation, because I don’t trust a product until I understand how it works.”

WATCHModel answer: TrueCar

Product or service: TrueCar, a free website for buying a car.
The ad wants its audience to sign up for free and use TrueCar to see real prices and get offers from dealers.
It uses Logos because it explains a clear, simple process: see what others pay, just sign up for free, then certified dealers… make you competitive offers.

Second appeal: Pathos the buyers’ happy reactions (Not bad. Very nice. This is the one.) make car shopping feel easy and stress-free. Ethos also works: the word certified makes the dealers sound trustworthy.

WATCHModel answer: POM Wonderful

Product or service: POM Wonderful pure pomegranate juice.
The ad wants its audience to buy and drink POM because it’s healthy.
It uses Logos because it makes a science-sounding health claim: the juice is full of antioxidants that fight free radicals.

Second appeal: Pathos humor. A burly battalion of free-radical-impaling antioxidants turns the juice into a tiny army. It’s funny and memorable, so you feel good about the brand.

Look closer: it sounds scientific, but there are no numbers and no source. In 2013, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission ruled that some of POM’s health claims in other ads were misleading. Sounding like science is not the same as evidence.

Exit ticket: a model

Your claim is your own, so there’s no answer key. Here is a model using the claim from the Week 9 model speech.

My persuasive claim: Employers should ask applicants for a short work sample, not just a resume.

EthosI’m a student who will apply for internships soon, and I’ll cite a published hiring study and say who ran it.
LogosA resume lists claims about your skills. A work sample shows them. Hiring research supports work samples as one of the stronger predictors of job performance.
PathosImagine being rejected for a job you could do well, just because your resume looked thin.
Check yours: Is each box about your claim and your audience? Can you name the actual source behind your logos? Is your pathos honest, not exaggerated? Bring this to the Persuasive Speech on Nov 24.
