Lecture

Daniel Susskind, “3 myths about the future of work (and why they’re not true)”
TED@Merck KGaA, Darmstadt · 2017 · about 16 minutes

Open the talk on TED (opens in a new tab)

Susskind is an Oxford economist. You have met his work before: Reading 4 cites his book A World Without Work (Susskind, 2020).

Before you start

  1. Do the pre-listening tasks in Tools and Templates (opens in a new tab) first. Predict what an economist will say about robots and jobs, and note what you already know from Readings 4 and 7.
  2. Learn these words before you press play. They carry the argument.
    • substitute (for): replace
    • complement: add value to, work together with
    • displace: push out of a place or role
    • encroach: move slowly into someone else’s area
    • routine / non-routine: can / cannot be explained as rules
    • fallacy: a common but mistaken belief
    • productivity: how much is produced per worker
    • GDP per head: a country’s output divided by its population
  3. Read every question before you press play. Underline the key words so you know exactly what to listen for.
  4. Set up your Cornell notes page. Tip: Susskind names three myths. Give each myth its own block on the page before you start.
  5. Print or open the paper and set aside 70 uninterrupted minutes.
  6. Play the talk once only. No pause, rewind, or second viewing.
Today’s focus: track the turn. For most of the talk Susskind sounds reassuring. Then he says, “That’s the troubling part. Let me say now why I think actually this is a good problem to have.” Strong listeners notice where a speaker’s position shifts, and write it down.
How this compares to the real exam. Same format, same one-viewing rule, same synthesis task. The real lecture is about 20 minutes, a little longer than this one, so leave space for a second Cornell page.

After you finish

  1. Replay any part you did not understand with subtitles on. Add what you missed to your Cornell notes in a different color, so you can see the gap between your first listen and the full picture.
  2. Check your work against the exemplar and mark scheme. Write your score out of 40 at the top.
  3. Look at the questions you lost marks on. Was it vocabulary, speed, numbers, or the structure of the argument? That is your target for Dec 1.
