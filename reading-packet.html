READING 01

What Counts as Work?

Week 1~950 words

What is work, and who decides which work is visible?

Ask most people to define work and they will describe a job: a place you go, an employer who pays you, an hourly rate or a monthly salary. This definition is useful because it is easy to measure. Governments count jobs. Economists count wages. Universities count graduate employment. But a definition that is easy to measure is not the same as a definition that is true, and the gap between the two shapes almost everything else in this course.

Consider the arithmetic. The International Labour Organization estimates that 16.4 billion hours are spent every day on unpaid care work (ILO, 2018). If that work were paid at minimum wage, it would represent roughly nine percent of global economic output.

Visible work and invisible work

Some work is recognized and some work is invisible, and the line between them is a social decision rather than a natural fact. Emotional labour, the effort of managing your own feelings and other people's in a service role, is work, and the sociologist Arlie Hochschild (1983) showed how systematically it goes unnamed.

A definition that is easy to measure is not the same as a definition that is true.

Social value and economic value

Economic value is what a market will pay for something. Social value is what a society gains from it. The anthropologist David Graeber (2018) argued that a significant number of modern jobs are experienced by the people doing them as pointless, while the jobs society genuinely depends on are underpaid.

Why this matters for you

Every prediction you will read this semester about automation rests on an underlying definition of work. Change the definition and the prediction changes with it. Your job, over the next fourteen weeks, is to become the kind of reader who notices which one an author has quietly assumed.

Your task. Write a 200–250 word paragraph that defines work more broadly than “paid employment only,” drawing on at least two ideas from this reading. Use one in-text citation.

READING 02

Learning for the Future Workplace

Week 2~900 words

If nobody can name the jobs of 2040, what exactly are we preparing for?

There is a claim you will encounter repeatedly: that most children entering primary school today will work in job types that do not yet exist. The World Economic Forum, often cited as its origin, has produced more careful analysis since (World Economic Forum, 2023). The habit worth developing is to notice how easily an unsourced statistic becomes common knowledge.

Job training optimizes for a known destination. Education optimizes for an unknown one. The OECD's Learning Compass 2030 frames this as developing student agency (OECD, 2019).

Tasks within jobs change faster than jobs disappear.

Three habits worth building deliberately

Learning to learn. Adaptability under incomplete information. Self-direction. Each is also a workplace competence with a different name: research, briefing, stakeholder communication, performance review.

READING 03

Future-Proofing: Skill, Adaptability, and the Long Career

Week 3~900 words3 citation fixes applied

What actually lasts when the job description keeps changing?

People now talk about portfolios, pivots, and lattices, a shift Arthur and Rousseau (1996) named the boundaryless career. The half-life of a skill has been shortening for specific tools (OECD, 2019). For underlying capacities such as structured reasoning, it has not shortened at all.

A career is now something you steer rather than something you climb.

The people who adapt best are usually those who have gone deep in at least one domain (Epstein, 2019), because depth teaches you what real understanding feels like. The T-shape model captures that adaptability is not the opposite of specialization.

Adaptability is a set of behaviours that can be trained. Portfolio Build 1 asks you to select two or three artifacts and explain what they show about your learning. Future-proofing is not a shield. It is a practice of staying learnable.

READING 04

Technology, AI, and the Changing Workplace

Week 4~950 words1 citation fix applied

How do we work now, and how might that change?

Automation means a machine performs a task a person previously performed. Augmentation means a machine changes how a person performs a task. A job is a bundle of tasks. The economist David Autor (2015) showed that automating some tasks can raise the value of those that remain.

A job is a bundle of tasks. Very few jobs are automated entirely.

Brynjolfsson and McAfee (2014) described the “second machine age” in which machines augment cognitive rather than physical labour. Three categories of work keep appearing where humans remain necessary: physical dexterity, genuine social judgement, and accountability. Even if a system produced better diagnoses than a doctor, the question of who is responsible when it is wrong does not disappear (Susskind, 2020). Accountability is a social requirement, not a technical limitation.

READING 05

Transferable Skills, Employability, and Academic English

Week 5~850 words1 citation fix applied

Why would an employer care what you learned in an English course?

Employers do not hire attributes. They hire evidence. Tomlinson (2017) calls this graduate capital. English for Academic Purposes is the study of how knowledge is constructed and communicated within a community (Hyland, 2006). Source selection becomes market research. Summarizing becomes the executive brief. Synthesis becomes analysis.

Employers do not hire attributes. They hire evidence.

Coxhead's (2000) Academic Word List identifies 570 word families that recur across disciplines. The category “soft skills” does real damage. A better name is evidence-based communication. Your portfolio is not a folder. It is an argument about your own development.

READING 06

Listening in the Academy: Selecting What Matters

Week 5~800 words2 citation fixes applied

In a fifty-minute lecture, how do you decide what to write down?

Most students who describe themselves as bad at academic listening are failing to select (Field, 2008). The problem is not comprehension. It is triage. Signposting phrases are the speaker telling you what to write down.

Signposting is the speaker telling you what to write down.

Working memory is doing two jobs at once during a lecture (Sweller, 1988). Writing everything consumes the same limited capacity that comprehension needs. The Cornell system, developed by Pauk and Owens (2013), separates these operations in time. On the second listen, mark difficult passages with a time stamp. Over eight videos, the pattern tells you whether your difficulty is vocabulary, speed, accent, or structure.

Your task. Separate key information from supporting detail in the supplied extract, then take Cornell notes on one full practice recording.

When two credible sources disagree, how do you decide?

Frey and Osborne (2017) estimated 47% of US employment at high risk. Arntz et al. (2016) estimated 9%. Same phenomenon, fivefold difference. The gap comes from a decision made before any data was analysed: what is the unit of analysis?

The gap comes from a decision made before any data was analysed.

Three questions for any prediction: What is being counted? What is the time horizon? Who produced this, and what follows for them if it is believed?

READING 08

Ethics, Citizenship, and the Social Impact of Technology

Week 8~950 words1 citation fix applied

Who benefits, who is harmed, and who gets to decide?

Ethics tends to arrive last. A better sequence starts with three questions at the design stage: who benefits, who bears the cost, and who is in the room when it is decided?

Who benefits, who bears the cost, and who is in the room when it is decided?

O'Neil (2016) argues algorithmic systems are especially dangerous when opaque, at scale, and producing feedback loops. The claim is about scale and opacity. Crawford (2021) documents the physical basis of AI: mineral extraction, energy, and low-paid human labour of data labelling. Eubanks (2018) documents a parallel pattern in public services, where algorithmic systems designed to reduce costs end up profiling and punishing the poor.

Communication is an ethical act. Selecting sources that support your position and omitting those that do not is misrepresentation. Attributing an idea to its source is the mechanism by which a listener can check you.

READING 09

Sustainability, Resilience, and Acting Under Uncertainty

Week 10~900 words1 citation fix applied

How do you make a decision when you cannot know the outcome?

The Brundtland Commission defined sustainability as development that meets present needs without compromising future generations (WCED, 1987). Resilience means the capacity to absorb a shock and continue to function, sometimes by changing structure (Masten, 2014). Endurance is holding the same shape until you break. Resilience includes changing shape.

Endurance is holding the same shape until you break. Resilience includes changing shape.

Taleb (2012) argues the best systems gain from shocks, a property he calls antifragility. Four principles: Prefer reversible decisions. Keep slack. Decide what would change your mind, in advance. Act, then revise. Each is executed through language.

Your task. Map your remaining deadlines, identify where you have no slack, and write two or three sentences explaining the relationship between resilience and sustainability.

READING 10

From Academic English to Professional Communication

Week 11~900 words3 citation fixes applied

What transfers out of this room, and how would you prove it?

Both academic and professional settings require you to make a claim, support it with evidence, and anticipate the strongest objection. These are, as Hyland (2006) argues, the whole activity of academic communication, and they transfer intact.

Compression is a skill, and it is harder than expansion.

Bhatia (2014) identifies three genuine differences: position of the conclusion (inverted in professional writing), tolerance for length (compression is harder than expansion), and form of the citation (professional settings still require sourcing). The Cornell note becomes meeting notes. The DEIP becomes a project retrospective.

Yancey (2009) showed that portfolios become genuinely powerful when they acquire an audience beyond the instructor. Portfolio Build 3 asks you to curate Showcase artifacts and synthesize all four CCD visits. The question is not “what did I do?” but “what does this show, to someone who was not here?”

Your task. Portfolio Build 3. Write a 400–500 word DEIP reflection that curates your Showcase artifacts and synthesizes all four CCD visits.
