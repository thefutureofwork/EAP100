Section B · 12 marks

B1 [4] The pie gets bigger (2): technology raises productivity, so incomes rise and demand grows (1); people displaced from tasks in the old pie can find tasks in the new, bigger pie (1). The ingredients change (2): people spend their income differently and want entirely new goods, so new industries, tasks and roles appear (1); for example, the shift from farms to factories to offices (1).
B2 [4] The intelligence myth is the belief that machines must copy the way humans think and reason in order to outperform them (1). Tasks that people can explain as rules were called routine and seen as automatable; tasks needing judgment, creativity or intuition were called non-routine and seen as out of reach (1). The myth is wrong because, with more processing power, data and better algorithms, machines now do tasks in an “unhuman” way (1). Example (1), either: the Stanford freckle system, which uses pattern recognition on 129,450 past cases with no medical understanding; or Watson, which won Jeopardy! without “knowing” it had won.
B3 [4] A dock worker used a machine to make washers and felt guilty, believing he was “taking away the work of another man” because there was a fixed lump of work (1). Schloss saw that higher productivity lowers prices, raises demand and creates more work, so the lump is not fixed: believing it is fixed is the lump of labor fallacy (1). The superiority myth (the “LOLFF”) accepts that the lump of work grows, but wrongly assumes that humans will be best placed to do the new tasks (1). As machines improve, they may take the new work themselves: “demand for tasks isn’t demand for human labor.” Accept the satnav example: it will help driverless cars, not drivers (1).

Exemplar (Section C)

In his 2017 TED talk, the economist Daniel Susskind argues that machines do not only replace workers; they also complement them. However, as machines become more capable, he believes the balance between these two forces will shift in favor of machines. For this reason, he concludes that the main future challenge is not producing enough wealth but sharing it, because work may stop being the way most people get “a slice” of the economic pie.

Reading 4 presents a similar idea from David Autor (2015), who shows that a job is a bundle of tasks and that automating some tasks can raise the value of the tasks that remain. Both writers agree that we should analyze work in terms of tasks, not whole jobs, and that technology can make human work more valuable. Susskind’s examples of a taxi driver using satnav and an architect using design software illustrate exactly Autor’s point.

However, there is a genuine tension. Autor uses this idea to explain why there are still so many jobs after decades of automation, which suggests that complementarity is a lasting force. Susskind accepts this history but calls the belief that humans will always be best placed to do the new tasks the “superiority myth.” In his view, the same process that created jobs in the past may now benefit machines instead of people.

In my opinion, Susskind’s argument is persuasive but partly speculative. He openly says we are on a “path” and are “not there yet,” so his conclusion is a prediction rather than evidence. Still, his question about how to share prosperity is one that policymakers should take seriously now.
